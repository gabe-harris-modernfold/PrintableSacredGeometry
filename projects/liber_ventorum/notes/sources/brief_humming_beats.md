For the two-person humming effect you described, the useful target is a **difference** of roughly 1–5 Hz between two comfortable notes—not one special frequency that “resonates your head.” That gives you one to five loudness pulses per second. For a physical buzz from your own humming, start with your natural, comfortable humming pitch rather than forcing a particular frequency. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

## For the throbbing “inside your head” effect

Here are example pairs—not medically optimized frequencies, just easy illustrations of the beat-frequency rule:

| First tone | Second tone | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 150 Hz | 151 Hz | One slow pulse per second |
| 150 Hz | 153 Hz | Three pulses per second |
| 200 Hz | 204 Hz | Four pulses per second |
| 250 Hz | 255 Hz | Five pulses per second |

The key is how far apart the notes are. You don’t specifically need 150, 200, or 250 Hz; choose a pitch both people can sustain comfortably. Close tones produce beating, while greater separation can make the sound fluttery or rough before the tones become more distinct. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

A practical experiment:
1. Both people hum a relaxed, sustained “mmm” at approximately the same pitch.
2. One holds steady while the other makes a very small upward or downward pitch adjustment.
3. Listen for slow swells, then a faster flutter as the pitches separate.
4. Keep the volume gentle rather than increasing it to intensify the effect. This combines the established beating mechanism with standard guidance to hum without strain. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

## For actual vibration in your lips, nose, and face

There isn’t an established universal “best head frequency” in the evidence here. Clinical humming guidance instead recommends a comfortable pitch, a relaxed jaw and tongue, and a smooth, gentle sound. You may feel vibration around the lips, nose, and cheeks without needing a loud or forceful hum. [cuh.nhs](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/humming-exercises/)

Try gently sliding your pitch up and down around your normal hum and noticing where the facial buzz feels clearest. Treat that as finding a comfortable sensation—not as measuring a single skull resonance. Your own voice also reaches you through bone conduction, which contributes to its internally felt quality. [cuh.nhs](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/humming-exercises/)

## Don’t chase stronger vibration with volume

A beat at 4 Hz is a four-times-per-second loudness pattern; it does not establish that your brain is mechanically vibrating at 4 Hz. Likewise, stronger sensation is not evidence of greater benefit. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

Avoid blasting tones through headphones or speakers to make your head shake. Sound level and exposure time determine hearing risk: repeated or prolonged exposure at or above 85 dBA can damage hearing, and louder sound can do so faster. Stop humming if you feel discomfort or tension, and stop the experiment if it produces ear pain, ringing, or dizziness. [cuh.nhs](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/humming-exercises/)
