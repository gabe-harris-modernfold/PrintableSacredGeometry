What makes it striking is that neither person needs to pulse their voice: both can hold steady tones, yet together they produce a throbbing sound. That emergent rhythm is called acoustic **beating**—a real fluctuation in the combined sound pressure reaching your ears. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

## 1. How two steady voices produce a pulse

Imagine one person humming at 200 cycles per second and the other at 204. The second person’s vibration is slightly faster, so the two waves continually slip past one another:

- When their pressure peaks arrive together, they reinforce each other, making the combined sound stronger.
- When one wave’s pressure peak arrives with the other’s pressure trough, they partially cancel, making it weaker.
- Because their frequencies differ, they repeatedly move into and out of alignment. [physicsclassroom](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/tutorial/sound-waves/properties-of-sound-waves/interference-and-beats)

It’s somewhat like two rotating wheels turning at slightly different speeds: their marked points repeatedly line up, separate, and line up again.

The beat rate is:

\[
f_{\text{beat}}=|f_2-f_1|
\]

So 200 Hz and 204 Hz produce four loudness pulses per second. You hear a tone around the original pitches with a “wah-wah-wah-wah” riding on it—not simply a new, standalone 4 Hz bass note. Beating is a form of amplitude modulation: the strength of the sound varies over time. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

## 2. Why it changes from a slow swell to a strange buzz

As the pitch difference changes, so does the experience:

| Two tones | Beat rate | Likely character |
|---|---:|---|
| 200 and 200.5 Hz | 0.5 pulses/second | One slow swell every two seconds |
| 200 and 202 Hz | 2 pulses/second | Clearly separated “wah-wah” pulses |
| 200 and 204 Hz | 4 pulses/second | Faster throbbing |
| Increasingly separated tones | Increasingly rapid fluctuations | Flutter or roughness, eventually more distinct pitches |

The beat rates follow the frequency-difference rule; the transition from distinct pulses to roughness depends on the pitches and the ear’s frequency resolution, rather than one universal cutoff. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

The inner ear separates sound using different regions of its basilar membrane. When two tones are close enough to stimulate overlapping regions, their interaction can sound fused and rough. Farther apart, they become easier to resolve separately. That’s why a small pitch adjustment can change the sensation dramatically—from smooth, to throbbing, to raspy or buzzy. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/cmns/Handbook5/handbook/Critical_Band.html)

## 3. Why two human voices can make it especially complex

The simple example assumes two pure sine waves. Voices are complex tones containing multiple frequency components, or partials. Their interactions can therefore be more complicated than one clean beat rhythm: different nearby components can contribute different fluctuations and roughness. Beating between upper partials is also part of the explanation for roughness in musical combinations. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/cmns/Handbook5/handbook/Critical_Band.html)

Consequently, two people may seem to hold almost the same note while the combined sound has a moving, textured quality rather than a perfectly regular pulse.

## 4. Does “inside my head” mean my skull or brain is vibrating at the beat rate?

Not necessarily. Acoustic beating describes a fluctuation in the sound reaching your ears, and auditory roughness describes how your hearing system responds to interacting tones. Neither observation, by itself, establishes that your skull or brain is mechanically resonating at that rate. [physicsclassroom](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/tutorial/sound-waves/properties-of-sound-waves/interference-and-beats)

An important distinction is:

- Interference: two waves combine, reinforcing and canceling.
- Beating: their slightly different frequencies make that reinforcement and cancellation repeat over time.
- Resonance: a system responds especially strongly around one of its natural frequencies.

The “wah-wah” mechanism is interference producing beating; it does not require head resonance. [physicsclassroom](https://www.physicsclassroom.com/tutorial/sound-waves/properties-of-sound-waves/interference-and-beats)

Likewise, a 4 Hz beat rate describes four loudness pulses per second. It is not, on its own, a measurement of your brain activity. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

## 5. The clearest identifying feature

The strongest clue is that the pulse slows as the two pitches approach each other, and disappears when the frequencies match. Move slightly away from that match and the pulse returns. Musicians use exactly this behavior to tune instruments: they listen for the beats to slow and vanish. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)

So, for your situation, the useful distinction is whether the “head vibration” tracks the difference between the two voices. If it speeds up and slows down with tiny changes in pitch, that strongly fits acoustic beating. [sfu](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/handbook/Beats.html)
