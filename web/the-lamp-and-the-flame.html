The Lamp and the Flame

how shared meaning changes hands — and how the change is made acceptable

People build shared meaning to coordinate their lives. Others gain influence by becoming its interpreters, protectors, sponsors or administrators — and that influence can redirect the system toward different ends. The original symbols may survive while the authority behind them changes.1

The central issue is legitimacy: people's belief that someone has the right to interpret, lead or govern. Capturing something already considered legitimate can be easier than persuading people to accept an entirely new authority — and ideologies can make a particular hierarchy look natural, proper or morally necessary.2

1 · Shared meaning

A community builds meaning, trust and practice to coordinate its life.

2 · Custodians

Interpreters, protectors, sponsors and administrators gain influence.

3 · Redirection

That influence can turn the system toward different ends.

4 · Same symbols

The symbols survive. The authority behind them has changed.

II · The reversal

When the means become the end

A community creates an organization to serve a purpose. Over time, serving the organization can become the practical definition of serving the purpose. Robert K. Merton called this goal displacement: something valuable as an instrument comes to be treated as valuable in itself.7 Theory

It begins with three things that, at the founding, look like one.

Those equivalences are not automatically true. They matter most when leaders control the very procedures members could use to challenge them — the advantage at the center of Michels' analysis.3 The first thing to track in any history: when did criticism of a particular arrangement begin to be treated as opposition to the underlying cause?

III · The slide

Six stages from shared value to self-sealing authority

The reversal does not happen in a single jump. It runs through a chain of dependencies, bargains, and changes in who gets to decide what counts as faithful service.

SynthesisMichels' and Selznick's organizational mechanisms combined with research on how ideology meets needs and legitimizes authority — a hypothesis to test against the evidence, not a historical law.1,3,6

STAGE 1

Shared value creates something worth controlling

A community develops trust, practices, relationships and shared meaning. Its shared narratives are not decoration: they tell people what membership, obligation and collective action mean.8 Think of the result as a stock of accumulated credibility. Someone acting alone must persuade others to listen; someone speaking from an established office can inherit attention, trust and access built by generations of others.

That makes the institution attractive to very different actors — a sincere organizer who wants it more effective, a sponsor who wants influence over what it does, an ambitious insider who wants a consequential position, an outside authority who wants a dependable partner. These are possible roles, not diagnoses of anyone's motives. Resource-dependence theory explains why such relationships matter even when everyone describes them as practical cooperation.9

What becomes contestable is not just a doctrine or an organization, but an already functioning relationship of trust.
STAGE 2

Necessary service creates dependency

Growth brings real coordination problems: someone must preserve records, distribute resources, resolve disputes, train successors and communicate decisions. Specialization solves them — and gives the people who operate the system knowledge, continuity and access that occasional participants lack. This is the core of Michels' account of how leaders entrench.3

Expertise answers: how can we accomplish this? Authority answers: who decides what we are trying to accomplish? Watch for the moment the first begins to confer the second.
Illustration  a mutual-aid association
  1. A treasurer becomes indispensable because the accounts are complicated.
  2. The treasurer gains influence over which projects are “financially realistic.”
  3. Financial realism becomes the main test for approving projects.
  4. The practical mission becomes whatever the finances can comfortably sustain.
No theft or malice is needed. Control over a means has become control over the ends.
STAGE 3

Dependency becomes bargaining power

Not every important contributor can redirect an organization. The question is whether the organization can afford to lose them. Resource-dependence theory treats power as relational: an organization's choices are constrained by whoever controls what it needs. One response is co-optation — bringing that source of constraint into governance, trading some independence for support.9 The support need not be money: legal permission or protection; access to audiences or distribution; buildings, land, equipment or archives; specialized knowledge; recognition by other authorities; recruitment and succession.

“Their contribution helps us” → “We cannot continue without their cooperation.” From there, a formally optional preference works as a veto. Judge independence by the real alternatives, not the written constitution.

The direction of capture is often ambiguous. A movement may believe it is recruiting a powerful sponsor; the sponsor may believe it is bringing the movement into a manageable relationship. Both can be partly right — as in Selznick's Tennessee Valley Authority.6

STAGE 4

Reasonable compromises change what gets selected

No one needs to announce a new mission. The organization changes which proposals succeed, which people advance and which disagreements receive attention — through the four gradual routes shown below.

Illustration  an educational movement
  1. A sponsor asks for predictable programming.
  2. Predictable programming favors standardized instruction.
  3. Standardization makes certain teachers easier to evaluate.
  4. Those teachers are promoted.
  5. Their successors inherit the evaluation system.
  6. The exploratory approach survives in the mission statement but becomes hard to practice.
Each decision is defensible in isolation. Together they change the kind of organization being reproduced — with no single moment when anyone agreed to abandon the founding purpose.
STAGE 5

Inherited language reconciles — or conceals — the change

Practical priorities change while the vocabulary stays. In Merton's account, following procedures becomes so important that procedures stop being judged against the purposes they were meant to serve; rules acquire an authority of their own.7 The tell is a conditional relationship hardening into an identity — the substitutions in Part II.

No deception is required: the revised reading can be sincerely experienced as fidelity.
STAGE 6

The system becomes hard to correct from within

The deepest change is not necessarily that leaders become personally corrupt. It is that the mechanisms for detecting and correcting departure come to depend on the very people or arrangements being questioned. Michels highlights leadership's organizational advantages; Merton shows trained adherence becoming rigidity. Together: a system can grow skilled at reproducing its own procedures while becoming less able to ask whether they still serve its purpose.3,7

The revealing issue is not whether disagreement exists, but whether it can produce consequential correction.

An institution may permit wide discussion while keeping appointments, budgets and binding interpretation beyond challenge. That is a possibility to investigate, not something to assume from the existence of hierarchy alone. The questions to ask are in the field kit.

Four routes of gradual change

Institutional research associated with Wolfgang Streeck, Kathleen Thelen and James Mahoney describes how substantial transformation can happen gradually.10,11 Theory

beforechange under wayafter
1Layering

New rules, offices or procedures are added alongside existing ones and gradually change how the institution operates.

Staysthe old rule, still in force
Changeshow the institution operates — through the added layer
2Conversion

Existing rules or institutions are interpreted and used for different purposes.

Staysthe rule itself, word for word
Changeshow it is interpreted, and what it is used for
3Drift

Formal rules remain, but changing external conditions alter their effects, because the rules are not adjusted.

Staysthe rule, and where it aims
Changesthe outside conditions — and with them, the rule’s effect
4Displacement

Existing arrangements are replaced by others, sometimes through a gradual process.

Staysnothing of the old arrangement
Changesthe arrangement itself, replaced by another

Unplanned transformation can run through any of the four. Drift here means only the specific mechanism — rules left unadjusted while conditions change — not any change nobody planned.

IV · Intent

Capture, accommodation — or both?

The contrast is not simply “conspiracy or accident.” In deliberate capture, actors set out to gain control. In emergent transformation, many individually defensible decisions add up to a result few participants explicitly planned. At least four patterns deserve separating.

VI · Casebook

Different routes to a similar outcome

These cases are not morally or historically interchangeable. Their value is that each reveals a different route by which control changes through the very arrangements meant to secure continuity or cooperation.

601

Gregory the Great's letter to Mellitus

Selective continuity · Documented

Gregory instructed that suitable pagan temple buildings in Britain be kept, their idols removed, and the buildings rededicated to Christian worship — partly so people could return to places already familiar to them.5

Familiarity and sacred geography can support a change of religious allegiance. It documents a strategy, not proof the strategy was carried out everywhere.

before 1914

German Social Democratic Party

Phase 4a · internal oligarchy · Theory

When Michels published his study in 1911, the SPD was a mass party on its way to roughly a million members by the eve of the First World War.30 It drifted from its radical roots toward caution as a professional staff took over.25 Michels argued that specialization gives leaders control over expertise, communication and training, letting them dominate the membership.3

A movement can come under insiders' control without being infiltrated by an outside group. Administrative necessity can become entrenched authority.

1930s–40s

Tennessee Valley Authority

Phase 4b · external co-optation · Theory

To secure local cooperation, the federal agency ran its farm program through the Farm Bureau and land-grant colleges. That informal accommodation gave established agricultural interests influence: the TVA adopted the outlook of larger, conservative white landowners and abandoned public land ownership.6,20

Co-optation runs both ways — the institution absorbs potential opponents, and they reshape it. An accommodation with consequences, not one actor's complete control of another.

1960s

Consciousness-raising groups

Phase 1 · structurelessness · Theory

The women's liberation movement's consciousness-raising groups worked well for open discussion — and broke down when they tried to take coordinated action.13

Without explicit structure, informal leadership goes unaccountable, and groups founder when their tasks outgrow their organization.

Stigler, 1971

US airline and trucking regulation

Phase 4c · regulatory capture · Theory

The Civil Aeronautics Board kept interstate fares above intrastate fares. The Interstate Commerce Commission's trucking rules protected the railroads.26

Regulation can be acquired by the industry it regulates — and, as the later deregulation showed, capture can be undone.

2001–2007

Wikipedia

Phase 2 → 3 · Documented

Active editors grew from hundreds in 2001 to a peak of 56,400 in March 2007, while implicit norms became formal policies enforced by bots and templates. Those quality controls ended up “crippling the very growth they were designed to manage”: newcomers were “rudely greeted by automated quality control systems” and “overwhelmed by the complexity of the rule system.”17

Codification can tip into rule displacement: the controls built to manage growth can choke it.

study, 2014

683 wikis

Phase 4a · Documented

As they grew, a small founding elite consolidated power.27

Michels' prediction holds even for open online communities.

VII · Field kit

Testing a real case

A tradition changing is not, by itself, evidence that it has been captured. Shared symbols do not, by themselves, establish a hidden line of control. Look for changes in power, not similarities in language or symbolism.

Did power change hands?

  1. Who could make binding decisions before and after the change?
  2. Who controlled appointments, funding, communication and training?
  3. What did new participants receive in exchange for cooperation?
  4. Which original commitments became inconvenient or unenforceable?
  5. Could ordinary members reject the change or replace its advocates?
  6. What evidence shows intention, as distinct from an unintended outcome?

These translate the research's emphasis on resources, leadership, legitimacy and bargaining into a historical test.4

Can it still correct itself?

  1. Can members inspect the evidence behind decisions?
  2. Can they organize an alternative leadership?
  3. Can they challenge an interpretation without forfeiting membership?
  4. Can failures count against the governing strategy?
  5. Can resources be redirected away from an established office?
  6. Who decides whether a complaint is legitimate?

Stage 6 in practice: disagreement is not the test — consequential correction is.

VIII · Resistance & renewal

The cycle is not inevitable

Elinor Ostrom found that long-lasting self-governed commons share common design features, each of which blocks a specific failure in the cycle.28 Theory

Clear boundaries
Rules that fit local conditions
Collective-choice arrangements: most people affected by the rules can help change themdirect counter to Phase 3
Monitors who answer to members
Graduated sanctions
Cheap local conflict resolution
Recognized rights to self-organize
Nested, multi-layer governance

Freeman's principles — rotate tasks, spread authority, share information widely — address Phase 1 and early Phase 4.13

Co-optation is not purely corrosive either. Couto's later study of the TVA treats it as a “changing balance” rather than a fixed outcome: co-opted groups can gain real power over an agency, and co-optation can work as participation for groups that otherwise have little power.29 Capture can also be undone: Stigler's analysis fed the bipartisan deregulation of the 1970s and 1980s.

And Phase 4 can lead back to Phase 1 through schism, reform movements or refounding. New groups form around the purpose the old institution abandoned, and the cycle starts again.

IX · The deepest root

Mistaking the lamp for the flame

The root vulnerability is not merely greed. It is attachment to the concrete things through which abstract values become accessible.

  • a truththrougha teacher
  • a communitythroughan institution
  • a traditionthroughan authorized interpreter
  • a causethrougha movement
  • a sense of securitythrougha leader

These relationships can be genuinely valuable. They also make it possible for loyalty to transfer from the value to its current custodian. Research on ideological needs explains the attachment; organizational research explains how custodians acquire durable power.1,3

The counterfactual test

If preserving the founding purpose required reducing the present leadership's power, funding or prestige, could the institution recognize that as success?

It is not a universal moral test — real trade-offs can be hard. But it sharply separates two objectives that institutional language often blends: preserving the purpose, and preserving the present arrangement. Follow the point where they begin to diverge, then ask who has the authority to say whether the divergence exists.

So the most productive question is not “Who stole whose story?” It is:

How did control over interpretation, resources and membership change — and how was that change made acceptable to the people involved?
