Language of the Plume

Learn to read the plume. Sit at a ridge-top table with a burning cone and watch. The table learns how you read, when you feel a presence in the smoke and when you feel only weather, and tells you, sitting by sitting, how your reading grows.

Readings are blind in the moment: the table never says what any one moment was, so the record holds how you read, not what correction taught you. At the end of each sitting it tells the sitting as a whole, and the presence score moves. That score is the heart of the practice: how well you tell a sent moment from plain weather, by feel.

The language

About the ridge
Ridge
The crest runs 9 to 3 o'clock and the slopes fall toward 12 and 6. You sit at 6.
Day
The sunlit south slope's upslope flow wins at the crest and pushes smoke toward 12.
Dusk
The hinge between breaths: weak, wandering air. The best time to be heard.
Night
Cold air drains off the crest down one slope, then the other.
Column
Most of the time the smoke stays smooth for only a few centimetres above the cone and is busy from there up. In a rare calm spell it climbs as one thin straight column, up to about two feet, then suddenly takes a new form: a sideways whip, a chain of puffs, a turbulent bloom, or two streams.
Air's arc
The bright stretch of the table's rim is where the ridge's own air has lately been carrying the smoke, so where a Stone would land most often if nobody steered it. It follows the natural wind only. A lean that holds against that arc is one the wind could hardly fake.
Fork
In a light crosswind, and late in the burn when the ember is a ring, the plume can split into two streams for a few seconds.
Late burn
The ash cap and the cooling cup make weak smoke that spills to every side on its own.
The smoke is a particle model riding a stochastic ridge wind (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck gusts, divergence-free eddies, a laminar column that breaks higher in still air). The reader model is fitted from your own readings by maximum likelihood with weak priors; the presence score is the signal-detection measure (d′ and criterion) of how well your felt sense separates a sent moment from plain weather. Whether anyone is sending is the question a reader trains to answer. People have read smoke this way for a very long time; this table is a place to practise.
