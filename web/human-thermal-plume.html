The Body's Own Weather

the plume you are standing in, simulated and measured
“For the Air is the body of the Soul, and the Soul rideth in it.” — CORPUS HERMETICUM X, TR. G. R. S. MEAD, 1906

A person standing still in a quiet room is not standing in still air. Skin runs several degrees above the room, the air touching it warms, becomes buoyant, and climbs — a sheet a few millimetres thick at the ankles, thickening as it rises, breaking into a turbulent column somewhere around waist height, and shedding discrete rings of rotating air above the head every few seconds. It is a weather system, roughly two metres tall, and it belongs to you. This page simulates it from measured boundary conditions, and traces it with the oldest instrument for the job: incense smoke.

The smoke is passive: particles are carried by the computed velocity field and do not push back on it. That is also true of real incense smoke at these concentrations, which is why it has been the standard tracer for this flow since long before anyone owned a laser.

Put the censer at the ankles and the smoke is captured almost immediately — it is drawn into the thin rising sheet on the skin and carried the whole length of the body. Put it by the hand and it joins higher up, where the layer is already thick and unsteady. Put it between the eyes and capture is instant: the brow sits inside the fastest, hottest part of the whole system, a few centimetres from skin running 35.0 °C, and within five seconds every grain of that smoke is above the crown — against none of the smoke lit at the ankles in the same five seconds. It does spread once it is up there, because the air above the head is slow and the column is done accelerating. Put it across the room and nothing is captured directly — but leave it running and the smoke creeps along the floor toward the figure, then turns upward at the ankles. A rising column has to be fed, and it draws its replacement air in along the floor. Smoke lit three-quarters of a metre away arrives at the feet, not at the height it was lit.

That last case is the one worth sitting with, because the simulation corrected the obvious guess. The reach of a thermal plume is not the distance it can pull smoke up from — it is the distance over which it quietly drags the whole floor-level air supply toward itself. Measured in this run, that inward creep runs at 0.043 m/s while the plume climbs about ten times faster, which is why it stays invisible until you put a tracer in it.

On the brow position. This is a two-dimensional slice taken looking at the figure head-on, so it has no “in front of the face” direction to put a censer in. The source sits on the brow line just clear of the traced head outline, which measures 8.8 cm from the midline at that height — as close to between the eyes as a front-facing slice can get.

What is actually driving it Measured

It is tempting — and wrong — to say the body's infrared radiation stirs the air. Radiation is real, and it is the larger of the two channels over the torso. But it does almost nothing to the air, because air is very nearly transparent at the 9–10 µm wavelengths skin emits. That energy crosses the room and is absorbed by walls, furniture, and other people. The air moves for a different reason: conduction across the few millimetres in contact with skin, and then buoyancy.

The cleanest way to see this is to plot what the skin actually emits. Below, Planck's law is evaluated live for skin at 310 K with an emissivity of 0.98 — the figure measured for a human hand — against a 298 K background, with the atmospheric transmission window shaded.

The peak sits at 9.35 µm, and it sits inside the window — which is the whole point, and the point cuts both ways. That band is called a transmission window precisely because the atmosphere barely absorbs there. It is why a thermal camera can photograph a hand across a room, why a hand can serve as a genuine incoherent infrared source, and, by exactly the same physics, why the 41% of the body's output that falls in that band reaches the walls rather than the air. The property that makes the body a usable infrared lamp is the property that stops it heating the medium in between.

The remaining 59% is worth being careful about rather than waving away. Outside the window, water vapour and carbon dioxide do absorb, so some of that fraction genuinely is taken up by air over a few metres of room. It still cannot drive this flow, for a reason that has nothing to do with how much energy it is: absorption along a metres-long path deposits heat diffusely through the whole room volume, not in the millimetres of air touching skin. Buoyancy needs a sharp local temperature gradient. A faint, uniform warming of the entire room produces no gradient anywhere, and therefore no plume. Convection wins not by carrying more energy — it does not — but by putting its energy in one thin, steep place.

Note also what kind of source it is. Emission from skin is incoherent and omnidirectional — a lamp, not a laser. There is no beam, no phase relationship, and nothing to converge. Whatever organised motion the body produces in the air around it, radiation contributes no momentum and no direction to it.

The pattern inverts what intuition suggests. Feet have the lowest radiative coefficient and the highest convective one; the chest and back have the opposite. A broad flat torso grows a thick, insulating boundary layer and sheds heat poorly by convection, while thin distal limbs sit at the cold base of the plume with a small characteristic diameter and shed heat efficiently. Only the hc column is what the simulation above computes. Everything in the hr column leaves the scene entirely and turns up as warm walls.

Those two datasets were measured by different people using different instruments, so it is worth checking they agree. Linearising the Stefan–Boltzmann law at these temperatures gives hr = 4εσTm³ = 6.2 W/m²K for a surface that radiates freely in every direction. The manikin measures 4.5 at the chest and 4.1 at the hand — ratios of 0.73 and 0.66. Those are effective radiating area fractions, and a standing human is quoted at about 0.70 to 0.73: limbs and torso partly face each other, so not all of the skin sees the room. The measured coefficients are exactly the blackbody figure times a plausible view factor, which is a good sign that the numbers on this page are describing the same person.

Why this distinction matters for the vortex question. Nobody has built a device that turns human infrared into a vortex, because there is no mechanism by which it would: the air the IR passes through barely absorbs it. What people have measured, repeatedly and carefully, is the buoyant plume — and that plume genuinely does shed rotating structures. The swirl is real. Its cause is a temperature difference and gravity, not radiation.

The ruler and the river Computed

A Rankine vortex is a mathematical idealisation: solid-body rotation inside a core radius a, and irrotational free-vortex flow outside it.

vθ(r) = Γr / 2πa²  for r < a    ·    vθ(r) = Γ / 2πr  for r > a

It is kinematics. It describes a velocity field that already rotates and says nothing about what set it rotating — which is why the same model gets applied to tornadoes, hurricanes, bathtub drains, and optical vortices in partially coherent light. Applying it to the human plume is holding up a ruler, not naming a cause. The offline study behind this page does exactly that, on the same criteria the experimental groups used: it computes the velocity-gradient tensor, isolates rotating cores with the swirling-strength criterion λci, and then fits each core's azimuthal profile twice — once with Rankine, once with the viscous Oseen vortex.

The textbook expectation is that Rankine should lose. Feed the same fitter a synthetic Oseen vortex of known circulation and it does, emphatically: Oseen recovers the circulation to 0.3% at R² = 1.000, while Rankine, given identical data and the same two free parameters, cannot beat R² = 0.87. Against an ideal viscous vortex, the corner really is fiction.

Then run it on the 695 cores this plume actually sheds, and the expectation inverts. Rankine is the better fit on 61% of them. Oseen keeps a higher mean R² (0.77 against 0.74) but that is a different claim: it earns the average by failing less badly on the worst cores, while on a typical core the kinked profile is closer. Their medians are effectively tied, 0.80 apiece.

The number that matters most, though, is that only one core in ten reaches R² = 0.9 under either model. Neither profile is the shape of a shed core, because a shed core is not a steady axisymmetric vortex — it is a lump of rotating fluid being stretched, tilted and carried away while you measure it. So the literature's “Rankine-like” comes out of this better than the clean theory predicts. As shorthand for near-solid-body rotation inside and decaying circulation outside, it is honest and it is usually the closer of the two. As a claim about the shape of any particular vortex, it was never going to survive contact with one. The ruler is the right length; nothing you measure with it has straight edges.

The Hermetic reading Tradition, not measurement

“That which is Below corresponds to that which is Above.”
THE EMERALD TABLET, AS RENDERED IN THE KYBALION, 1908

Nothing in this section feeds a number into the simulation above, and nothing above is evidence for anything below. Kept separate, on purpose.

The Hermetic tradition liked the correspondence between the body and the cosmos, and the thermal plume is an unusually literal instance of it: the same buoyant convection that lifts a thunderhead lifts the air off your shoulders, differing in scale by four orders of magnitude and in mechanism not at all. A person is a heat engine standing in a fluid, and the fluid organises itself around them into a column with a rotating top. The tradition's image of the body as a small world with its own airs and its own weather is, in this one narrow respect, a decent physical description rather than a metaphor.

The censer between the eyes sits where the tradition puts the inner eye, and it is the one position where the smoke goes almost straight up. That is worth saying plainly in both registers at once, because they do not agree about why. The reason the smoke behaves that way is that the brow is a few centimetres beneath the warmest skin on the body, inside a boundary layer that has been accelerating since it left the floor. The tradition's interest in that point long predates anyone measuring the temperature there, and the measurement does not vindicate the tradition — it just happens that the hottest part of a standing body is also the part the tradition looked at hardest.

The pyramid on the crown, and why it nearly works

The Pyramid overlay button puts a Great Pyramid section on the top of the head: an isosceles triangle whose faces meet the base at 51.84°, the slope a seked of 5½ palms per cubit gives, and the one the monument actually has to within a few arc-minutes. It is scaled to head width — about 20.5 cm across and 13.0 cm tall — because the head is the only natural scale on offer. Nothing about it enters the simulation. It is drawn on top of a finished frame and could be deleted without changing a single number.

The overlay also does something the symbol alone cannot: it puts the pyramid's slope next to the plume's measured slope, in the same units, on the same screen. And the answer is uncomfortably close. The pyramid's face stands 38.2° off vertical. The plume above the head, measured live as the second moment of its temperature field against height, spreads at roughly 33°. Five degrees apart. If you wanted to claim the crown's plume rises in the pyramid's angle, this is where you would stop looking.

So here is why that would be a mistake, and it is worth being specific rather than merely sceptical. First, the number is not stable. The offline study at Rayleigh 10⁷ — a hundredfold closer to a real body than the browser run — measures 15.3°, not 33°. Same physics, same method, less than half the angle. A quantity that halves when you improve the simulation is not a quantity that has agreed with anything. Second, the plume has no apex. A pyramid rises from a point; the plume leaves the crown already 20–30 cm wide, because it has been fed by the whole body on the way up. Two shapes can share a slope and still be nothing alike. Third, the angle is a choice. "Where the plume ends" is a threshold someone picks; move it and the angle moves with it. Classical plume theory, for what it is worth, puts a real turbulent plume nearer 6–15°.

That is three independent reasons the resemblance is empty, and it took a measurement to produce any of them. The overlay is here because a coincidence you can put a number on is far more useful than one you can only argue about — and because the honest result of measuring this particular correspondence is that it dissolves the moment you measure it twice.

Where it stops being a decent description is the moment the correspondence is read backwards — as though the resemblance were evidence of a shared cause, or as though naming the shape “Rankine” explained why it turns. It does not. The vortex above your head is made by warm air and gravity, and it would form identically above a bucket of water at 33 °C.
