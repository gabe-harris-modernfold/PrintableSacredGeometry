The Shadow Solids

Five Platonic solids, five archetypes, each with two faces: the one you live from when you feel whole, and the one that takes over when you are afraid.

This is a working table for psychological practice, built from three things you can check: the geometry of the five Platonic solids, the archetypes of Jung’s analytical psychology, and the senses as they meet you now — LED colour temperatures, synth waveforms, phone glass, a weighted blanket. Each solid holds one archetype with an integrated face and a shadow face. The anchors help you notice which one is running, and give you something concrete to shift it with.

What this table leaves out No letters, paths or spheres from the older systems; no planets, metals, gods or classical incense. The quality of each solid is read from its geometry alone — its faces, corners, symmetry and its dual — and every object and sense anchor comes from ordinary life in 2006–2026.

Why this order

07How the geometry mirrors the growth

Connections only grow. Counted by edges — the links between corners — the five climb 6, 12, 12, 30, 30 and never fall back. Development here is not adding parts but adding relationships between them.

One seed, then two mirrors. The tetrahedron is its own dual: put a point in the middle of each face and you get a tetrahedron again. That is the Child, who meets only itself. After it the solids come in dual pairs. The cube’s six faces become the octahedron’s six corners: the persona turned outward and the inner other carried within, which Jung described as complementary to the persona. The icosahedron’s twelve corners become the dodecahedron’s twelve faces: the Sage, who knows about wholeness from twenty viewpoints, and the Self, which is it. The inflated guru is the Self’s counterfeit — its mirror image, mistaken for the original.

Jung’s own sequence. In his 1928 essay The Relations between the Ego and the Unconscious the stages run persona, anima and animus, the mana-personality, then the self. Put the Child (his 1940 essay) at the start as the seed, and the five solids line up with them one to one.

The faces change kind only twice. Triangles — the smallest rigid face — carry the spark, the mirror and knowledge. Squares carry structure. Pentagons appear only at the end, and they carry the golden ratio (a pentagon’s diagonal over its side is φ = 1.618…), a number no fraction can write. The last form is built on a proportion that never closes: wholeness is not a finished state.

Round is not the same as whole. By surface against volume the icosahedron is the roundest of the five (sphericity 0.94 against the dodecahedron’s 0.91): the Sage presents the smoothest surface. But the dodecahedron fills more of the sphere through its corners (66% against 61%): the Self holds more. Note the one dip, cube to octahedron (37% to 32%). Turning to the inner other first feels like losing solid ground.

These are correspondences. They hold because the pattern is useful to work with, not because geometry causes the psyche; the numbers are exact, the readings are offered.

A reflective practice, not therapy. Shadow work can raise old pain. If an exercise brings up trauma, panic, or thoughts of harming yourself, stop, put the table down and talk to someone qualified.

Sound plays only when you press a button, and quietly. The traits are written for recognising patterns in yourself, not for diagnosing anyone else.

Where the geometry is exact, it is computed. Where it is read as a psyche, that is a reading.
