▶ What a Rodin coil actually is physics

Conventionally, this is an air-core toroidal transformer with an unusual winding path. Two conductors (bifilar) are laid diagonally across a torus following the {12/5} star pattern — each wire makes 12 poloidal wraps for every 5 trips around the ring, skipping 5 of the 12 poles (150°) each step.

Because the wires cross the surface diagonally rather than wrapping perpendicular like a normal toroid, the coil behaves like a toroid on its outer edge and a solenoid through the center hole. That hybrid geometry is the whole point — it lets field escape into the aperture instead of being fully confined to the core. Everything the simulation draws is computed from these actual wire paths, nothing else.

Geometry fixed to the study document's published dimensions: outer Ø 9.95 cm, aperture Ø 2.9 cm → major radius R = 32.1 mm, minor radius a = 17.6 mm.

▶ Biot–Savart: how the field is computed math

The magnetic field at any point is the exact sum of contributions from every short segment of both wires:

B(r) = (µ₀/4π) · Σ I·dl × (r − r′) / |r − r′|³

Each winding is discretized into hundreds of straight segments dl. For the cross-section heatmap this is evaluated on a 120×80 grid; for the 3-D field lines it's integrated with an RK-2 (midpoint) streamline tracer that steps along the local B direction. This is the same law FEM tools solve — the difference is method (see the FEM-comparison panel).

Near a wire the 1/r² term diverges, so we apply a ~1.2 mm softening radius (a finite-wire-thickness stand-in). That's why on-conductor values read lower here than in a meshed FEM solve.

▶ Why "series aiding" shows no external field physics

This surprises people. When both windings drive current the same way around the ring, the coil is a near-ideal toroid, and Ampère's law confines essentially all flux inside the donut core:

B_inside = µ₀NI / 2πr     B_outside ≈ 0

There are no poles and no external arcs to draw — not a bug, it's the defining property of a toroid. The simulator detects this by computing the coil's net magnetic dipole moment and, when it's near zero, prints "flux is confined inside the toroidal core" instead of drawing phantom field lines.

A bar-magnet picture (N/S poles + arcs) only appears when the winding has a net dipole moment — which happens in the opposing, anti-parallel, and single-winding modes.

▶ N / S poles & the dipole moment math

The poles aren't drawn arbitrarily — they're placed where the coil's computed magnetic dipole moment points. The moment is summed directly from the wire geometry:

m = ½ Σ w · (r × dl)    (w = winding current sign)

If |m_z| exceeds ~12% of a reference single-loop moment, the coil is "magnetized" and N (red) / S (blue) badges are placed on the axis. In opposing mode the measured moment is ≈ 0.0375 A·m² per amp — matching the theoretical value for the coil's 5 net axial current loops.

▶ The center point: max field, zero force physics

The exact center — the midpoint between N and S — is the point you asked most about. Two facts:

  • It is a field maximum: B₀ is strongest here and points purely along the axis (S→N).
  • It is a force equilibrium: ∂B/∂z ≈ 0, so a small magnetic object feels no net pull there. Magnetic force needs a gradient: F ∝ ∇(B²).

The steepest gradient — where the "grip" is strongest — sits off-center (≈ 14 mm up the axis at default settings). An object nudged off the midpoint gets pulled back toward it. The force tiles scale with current², so raising wattage 100× raises the pull 100×.

The 1 mm-steel-sphere force uses the small induced-dipole model (µᵣ≫1, object ≪ field scale) — good for probes and particles. A large iron core would itself reshape the field (needs FEM).

▶ The vortex: rotating field & spinning magnets physics

This is the effect the coil is famous for. Drive the two windings out of phase (the φ slider) and they act like a two-phase motor stator. The combined field in the aperture doesn't just oscillate — its pattern physically rotates:

B(t) = B_A·i(ωt) + B_B·i(ωt − φ)

Rotation is maximal at φ = 90° (quadrature) and vanishes at φ = 0° (pure bucking — oscillation only). The simulator's harmonic detector finds the dominant rotating component is the m = 12 azimuthal harmonic — the strand pitch of the 12-pole winding — so the pattern turns at ω/12, giving a sync speed of 5·f RPM. At the 20–200 Hz experimenters use, that's 100–1000 RPM — exactly the ball-magnet spin rates seen in videos.

Important nuance: the rotation is not at the dead center. By 12-fold symmetry the field there is axial and static in direction. The swirl lives near the aperture wall, where the strand ripple survives (it decays toward the axis as r¹²). A finite-sized ball spins because it couples to that near-wall rotating pattern.

▶ Frequency, wattage & the drive circuit math

The 3-D view's brightness, line weight, and flux-dot speed all scale with the real RMS current, which comes from a full AC circuit solve:

Z(f) = R_AC(f) + jωL    I = V / |Z|    P = I²·R_AC

In wattage mode the app inverts this — it solves for the drive voltage that hits your target power exactly (P ∝ V², so one unit-solve scales analytically).

R_AC(f) rises with frequency via skin and proximity effects, using the full Dowell equation:

δ = √(ρ/πfµ₀)    R_AC/R_DC = F_R(Δ),   Δ = (√π/2)(d/δ)

Non-sine waveforms are decomposed into a Fourier series and each harmonic sees its own Z(nf), so a square or PWM drive correctly shows extra high-frequency loss and a filtered current shape.

Field shape is deliberately independent of drive level — Maxwell's equations are linear in current, so only field strength changes with V/f/watts. Geometry changes only when you alter the winding connection.

▶ Bifilar connection modes how to
  • Series opposing — windings buck; net dipole → clear N/S poles + external arcs. Default; best "field" view.
  • Series aiding — windings add; near-ideal toroid, flux confined to core, no external field.
  • Anti-parallel bifilar — the study document's near-zero-inductance case; far-field cancels, local inter-wire shear remains.
  • Single winding A — one conductor only; simplest dipole, closest to a same-direction hand-wound coil.
  • Vortex (two-phase) — independent phase-shifted drive; unlocks the φ slider and the rotating-field / spinning-magnet demo.

Inductance follows the connection: aiding 2L₁(1+k), opposing 2L₁(1−k), anti-parallel L₁(1−k)/2, where the coupling k comes from the Neumann double-integral over the two real wire paths (default measured k ≈ 0.11).

▶ How this compares to COMSOL / FEM caveat

Validated against FEM results, the physics and scaling laws agree; differences are method-level:

  • Field topology matches — hot conductor bundles, moderate aperture, dark exterior equator, dipole far-field decay converging to µ₀m/2πz³ (within a few % by z = 20 cm).
  • FEM meshes the copper volume, so its on-conductor surface values are more trustworthy than this filament model's (which softens within ~1.2 mm of a wire).
  • FEM resolves current redistribution inside the conductor at high f; here skin/proximity enter only the circuit resistance (Dowell), not the field-map shape.
  • FEM gives one operating point per solve; this wraps the field solver in a live drive circuit (f, waveform, wattage) updating in real time.

Use the "linear colour scale (FEM-style)" checkbox on the cross-section to match FEM's linear |B| plots (default here is log, showing 3 decades). For final absolute numbers on conductor surfaces, the FEM remains the reference — matching the study document's own recommendation.

▶ Vortex-Based Mathematics: lore vs. physics caveat

The winding path genuinely follows the VBM 1-2-4-8-7-5 doubling sequence mapped onto the 12 poles — and that geometry is exactly what produces the diagonal, semi-open field behaviour. So the geometry from VBM is real and is fully modeled here.

However: the claim that the "3-6-9 gap" is a special magnetic channel does not enter the field equations anywhere. Everything the simulation shows — the aperture concentration, the vortex, the poles — emerges from Biot–Savart over the copper alone. The center concentrates flux because it's a solenoid-like axis, not because of numerology.

This simulator treats the coil as what mainstream physics says it is: a cleverly-wound air-core toroid. No over-unity, no free energy — power in always equals copper loss plus stored reactive energy returned each cycle.
