# Reading the smoke: libanomancy, capnomancy and their relatives

Research notes behind the lens dictionaries in `web/language-of-the-plume.html`. Compiled 2026-11-03;
widened the same day to Temple, Roman, Renaissance, signalling and scientific readings (sections 6–11).

What is well attested: a small Old Babylonian omen literature that reads the smoke and flame of an aromatic
offering by direction, shape and movement (two tablets, edited in the 1960s and 1980s); a Greek habit of
reading burnt offerings, including a named exception for frankincense; an early-modern European word,
*capnomancy*, with a generic rule (straight thin smoke good, heavy hanging smoke bad) that is repeated from
1880 onward; a Chinese folk practice whose printed charts read stick heights rather than smoke, with smoke
rules circulating only in modern compendia; and a Tibetan category of "smoke signs" described by an
ethnographer without its rules. What is not attested: any ancient Greek use of the word *kapnomanteia*, any
Druid smoke-reading, and most of the detailed "meanings" on modern web pages. Where a rule below rests on a
tertiary source or on a translation seen only second-hand, the Sources table and the `note` field say so.
The second pass adds one more well-attested direction rule (the Temple woodpile smoke in the Talmud), one
literary Roman portent (Seneca), the Renaissance -mancy catalogues, smoke used as an agreed signal rather
than an omen, and the scientific line from Leonardo to the buoyant-plume models of the 1950s.

## 1. Old Babylonian libanomancy (smoke omens)

Two cuneiform tablets from southern Babylonia, Old Babylonian period (roughly 1900–1600 BC), record omens
taken from the *qutrēnu*, the aromatic burnt offering. Pettinato published the first in 1966 (S2); Biggs
added a note in RA 63 (1969); Finkel published a new piece, UCLM 9-2433, in 1983/84 and collated it with
the earlier material (S1); Leichty later printed a short "Smoke Omens" note. Cingolo's 2016 survey (S3)
treats these as the only two known Old Babylonian libanomancy texts, places them among the activities of
the *bārû* diviner, and notes that Pettinato rendered *qutrēnu* as "incense" while Leichty and Biggs chose
"smoke" and she herself prefers "(aromatic) burnt offering". Koch's 2015 handbook (S4) also covers the
genre; I could confirm that only through reviews, not from the section itself.

The omens are conditional sentences: if the smoke does X, then Y. Renderings that circulate online (S5, S6)
are credited to Finkel's edition and include smoke drifting to the right (you will defeat your enemy), the
flame burning smokily when the offering is sprinkled (your army defeats the enemy), the smoke spreading
evenly on all sides (an even contest), clustering (prosperity), a top split in two, rendered in one place as
"If the top of the incense smoke is cleft (in two): madness" (S5), fragmented tops (losses), smoke that
tightens (hardship), and smoke that after a while pushes through and gets out (the client pulls through).
Other protases reportedly send the smoke to the east or toward the diviner's lap. I could not reach Finkel's
article or Cingolo's chapter text, so every rendering above is second-hand and marked uncertain in the JSON.

Seat convention. The tablets say little about procedure (S3). The protases themselves place the diviner
seated facing the censer, since some describe smoke moving toward his body; the "right" and "left" of the
texts are written as "its" right and left, and whether that is the diviner's side or the censer's is not
settled in anything I could read. Mesopotamian extispicy treats the right as the client's side and the left
as the enemy's, and the right-drift omen fits that pattern, but the libanomancy texts do not spell it out.
Tablet damage means the back of UCLM 9-2433 is lost; translators differ in wording and in a few readings.

Sitting with this lens: sit square to the cone with the censer directly in front, and take right and left
as your own. Watch for a sustained lean, for a top that splits, and for smoke that pours out on every side;
those are the three cues the tablets clearly use.

## 2. Greek kapnomanteia and libanomanteia

Strictly: the Greek compounds are not attested in antiquity so far as I could check. The Suda has no
headword in *kapnom-* (search run on the Suda On Line), Wiktionary has no Greek-script entry for either
word, and I could not reach LSJ. The words enter Western languages as Latin *capnomantia* in Renaissance
lists: Vives's commentary on Augustine, Englished by Healey in 1610 as "by smoake, Capnomancy" (S10), and
Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia* (1533), whose 1651 English version says capnomancy is "so called from
smoak" and searches into the flame and smoke (S9). The modern claim that Greek priests performed capnomancy
over sacrificial smoke, with thin straight smoke favourable, appears in nineteenth- and twentieth-century
reference works (S12, S13) and on web pages that cite only each other.

What the ancient texts do attest is reading burnt offerings. Diogenes Laertius (8.20) says Pythagoras
divined by sounds and auguries, "never by burnt-offerings, beyond frankincense" (S7), which is the one
classical statement that frankincense smoke could be read. Sophocles, *Antigone* 1005–1011, has Teiresias
report a sacrifice at which no flame rose and the thigh-fat oozed, "smoked, and sputtered" (S8), a sign that
the gods refuse the city. Neither passage gives a dictionary; the Sophocles sign is simply that smoke
without flame is bad. Beerden's 2013 study of Greek divination (not read; abstract only) notes that in
Greece and Mesopotamia incense could be both offering and sign, and that the sources do not say which.

Sitting with this lens: there is no seat to inherit; the reader simply watches whether the cone burns clean.
A column that stands and draws is the offering taken; a plume that sinks and sprawls without rising is the
Antigone sign.

## 3. Chinese incense reading

The printed charts (二十四香谱, 七十二香谱) read the relative heights of three sticks after they have burned
for a while, not the smoke; each height pattern has a name and a verdict (S15, S16). Baidu's entry calls the
practice a folk custom, says the verdicts have no scientific basis, and attributes the chart's origin to
unnamed Qing-period folk writings; I found no scholarly treatment of the chart's history, and the
scholarly "香谱" of the Song period (Hong Chu's) is an incense-recipe book, not a divination chart.

Smoke rules do exist, but only in modern folk compendia (S15), which name no book. The article I read gives:
smoke that rises thin and 直冲云天 like a hanging pagoda means a spirit attends the altar; smoke that swirls or
spins like a whirlwind means something adverse or an unquiet spirit; blue or white smoke is normal and
auspicious, black smoke unfavourable; several streams joining mean harmony; streams splitting in opposite
directions mean separation; black smoke from the centre of the stick means an unclean site. It mentions a
"真香图谱" that combines stick heights, smoke and colour, which I could not locate. These are the only smoke
rules carried into the JSON, marked tertiary.

Sitting with this lens: the reader faces the altar; direction is not part of these rules, so the seat does
not matter. Watch the column's shape and colour: a still straight column, a twisting one, or one that forks.

## 4. European capnomancy as catalogued

John Gaule's *Pus-mantia* (1652) lists "Capnomancy, by smoak" next to libanomancy by burning frankincense
(S11, ch. XIX, pp. 165–166); it catalogues the word without describing a method, and is preceded in English
by Healey's 1610 Vives (S10). McClintock and Strong (1880) supply the generic rule, calling it a species of
divination of the ancient heathen at sacrifice: thin light smoke rising straight is favourable, thick dark
smoke hanging over the fire like a cloud is unfavourable (S12). Spence (1920) repeats it: smoke from the
altar rising lightly and going "straight to the clouds" is a good omen, smoke that hangs about a bad one, and
adds two variants, jasmine or poppy seed thrown on coals and breathing the smoke of the sacrificial fire
(S13). None of these cites an ancient text.

Druids. The Druid or Celtic version (oak and mistletoe smoke, "dendromancy") rests, in Wikipedia's own
citations, on pagan-press books of 2002–2006 (S14); Spence's entry does not mention Druids, and I found no
earlier source. Treat it as modern lore. The same applies to the Semang and "Babylonian cedar" claims there.

Sitting with this lens: no seat; the rule is about the column alone. A straight, thin, rising column is the
favourable sign; smoke that sinks, spreads or will not rise is the unfavourable one.

## 5. Other traditions

Tibet. Ekvall's ethnography (S17, p. 271) lists *dud brda*, "smoke signs", as a subcategory of divination:
only the smoke of *bsang* offerings (juniper boughs, fragrant herbs) counts; its colour, volume and
"drift—direction, height, and spread" are read; the most elaborate readings come from the patterns seen as
the smoke nearly vanishes against the sky; the lore belongs to specialists. No rules are given, so the lens
is empty. The modern *Anthropological Forum* paper on *bsang* smoke (Tan 2018) concerns pollution, not omens.

India. Varāhamihira's *Bṛhat Saṃhitā* 43.31–37 (6th c. AD, Iyer's translation, S18) reads the sacrificial
fire at the Indra-banner rite: fragrant, glossy, full-flamed fire of auspicious shape is good; flames
"turning to the right" at the final oblation mean the king conquers; colour, sound, shape and smell follow;
verse 37 extends the omens to journeys, campaigns and marriages. The verses read flame, not smoke, so the one
entry is approximate. Varāhamihira's *Yogayātrā* has a fire-omen chapter (8) that I could not read.

Zoroastrian fire. Nothing sourced was found on reading smoke or flame as a sign; the literature I could
reach treats fire as object of worship, not as oracle.

## 6. The Temple smoke in the Talmud

The Babylonian Talmud (Bava Batra 147a, repeated at Yoma 21b) records, in the name of Rabbi Yitzhak bar
Avdimi, that at the close of the last festival day of Sukkot everyone watched the smoke rising from the
woodpile on the altar (S19, S20). The William Davidson translation opens the first case with "If the smoke
inclined toward the north due to a south wind" (S19): then the poor were glad and the householders sad,
because the rain would be plentiful and stored produce would rot. Toward the south (a north wind): the
reverse, sparse rain but produce that keeps. Toward the east (a west wind): everyone glad, enough rain and
good keeping. Toward the west (an east wind): everyone sad, drought. No upright or broken case is given.
The reading falls at the turn of the rainy season and treats the night wind as the year's rain.

Seat convention, and the mapping is mine. The text names compass points, not the watcher's sides. I assume
a watcher in the Temple court standing east of the altar and facing west toward the sanctuary, which is the
conventional orientation of the Temple; I did not check that orientation against a source in this pass.
Then west = away, east = toward, north = right, south = left. A reader who places the watcher elsewhere must
rotate the four entries. The time is the evening at the end of the festival; the JSON leaves `env` unset so
the lens can fire at any hour, and says so in each note.

Two related passages are rites, not omens. Yoma 53a: the high priest's Yom Kippur incense must contain the
*ma'aleh ashan*, a smoke-raising herb, so that the column rises straight like a staff to the roof and then
spreads; leaving it out is a capital offence, and the passage turns on the Sadducee view that the incense
should be lit outside the Holy of Holies (S21). Yoma 21b also lists, among the Temple wonders, that the wind
did not scatter the altar smoke: it swayed like a palm but did not disperse (S20). Exodus 13:21–22 gives the
pillar of cloud by day and fire by night that went before the people (S22): guidance, not a reading rule.

Sitting with this lens: sit facing the cone as if you stood east of the altar, so the far side of the table
is west. Read only the lean of the column, once, at the close of the session.

## 7. Roman fire and smoke

I searched Cicero's *De divinatione*, Pliny's *Natural History* and Seneca's *Natural Questions* for a rule
about smoke and found none in the passages I could reach; Roman divination as those authors present it reads
entrails, birds and lightning, and the Etruscan doctrine Seneca reports is about lightning. This is not a
full reading of the three works. The one Latin text that reads smoke in detail is a tragedy: Seneca's
*Oedipus* (c. 303–330, S23). Manto describes Tiresias's sacrifice to her blind father: the flame leaps and
falls, runs dark blue flecked with yellow, then blood-red, then splits into two; the smoke does not rise but
settles on the king, "stifling smoke surrounds the royal head" (Miller), and shuts out the light from his
face. Commentators read it as marking Oedipus as the polluted one and foreshadowing his blindness (secondary,
not read). It is a literary portent, not a manual, and the lens says so.

Sitting with this lens: watch whether the column, instead of climbing, bends toward you and settles. In
this reading the matter then rests with the one the smoke finds.

## 8. The named -mancies in the Renaissance lists

Rabelais, *Tiers Livre* (1546) ch. 25, has Her Trippa offer Panurge some thirty methods, a parody of Agrippa.
In the Urquhart–Motteux English, capnomancy is "the gallantest and most excellent of all secrets", and the
list also gives libanomancy (a little frankincense), tephromancy (ashes) and pyromancy (S25). Sideromancy,
daphnomancy and causimomancy were not in the parts of the chapter I could read. Rabelais does stage a laurel
reading in ch. 17–18: the Sibyl of Panzoust burns laurel, it makes no crackle, and Epistemon calls that a
dismal omen (S24). Modern lists define sideromancy as burning straw on iron, daphnomancy as burning laurel,
causimomancy as divination by burning and knissomancy by incense (S26), with no early source; I found nothing
that describes reading the figures or smoke of sideromancy, so that detail is unverified.

Martín Del Río's *Disquisitiones magicae* (1599–1600) is cited for libanomancy by modern references via
Maxwell-Stuart's translation, p. 160 (S6); I did not read it. I could not confirm that Kaspar Peucer's
*Commentarius de praecipuis divinationum generibus* (1553) treats smoke at all: the descriptions I found list
lots, dreams, weather, physiognomy, astrology and portents. Bouché-Leclercq's *Histoire de la divination
dans l'antiquité* (1879–82, S43) is online, but I did not reach any pages on smoke or incense.

## 9. Smoke as signal

These are codes agreed in advance, not omens, so they carry no lens.

Greece. Polybius 10.43–47 (Paton, S27) says early fire-signals were simple beacons that could only carry
pre-arranged news; Aeneas Tacticus improved them with paired water vessels whose graduated rods carried set
messages; Cleoxenus and Democleitus, refined by Polybius, spelled words with torches, five tablets of five
letters. Polybius speaks of torches and fire, not of daytime smoke.

China. Chinese reference pages (S29, seen only through search summaries) relay the Han distinction between
*feng*, fire by night, and *sui*, smoke by day, from Li Xian's commentary on the *Hou Hanshu*; they trace the
claim that *langyan* (wolf smoke) was made by burning wolf dung to Tang writings such as the *Youyang zazu*,
and doubt it, since excavated beacon fuel is tamarisk, reed and brush. Wikipedia adds that smoke colour on
the Great Wall told the size of the enemy force (S28), cited to non-specialist works.

Plains. Wikipedia (S28) describes damp grass fired on a rise, with smoke from halfway up meaning all was well
and from the top meaning danger, but cites only a web page. W. P. Clark's *Indian Sign Language* (1885) is
said to list smoke among signalling methods; I located the scan but could not reach the entry.

The conclave. Ballots are burned after each vote. Per Frederic Baumgartner, relayed by *U.S. Catholic* (S30),
the first intentional white smoke announced Benedict XV in 1914; black smoke came from wet straw and later
from chemicals. Since 2005 a second stove makes the colour, and in 2013 the Vatican gave the recipes:
potassium perchlorate, anthracene and sulphur for black, potassium chlorate, lactose and rosin for white (S31).

## 10. Other regions checked

Mesoamerica: copal is well described as an offering; the only smoke-reading claim I found (Otomi healers
diagnosing from smoke patterns) is on a vendor blog with no citation, so it is left out. Andes, the North
American pipe and smudging, Arabic *bukhūr*, Siberian and Mongol fumigation: nothing sourced was found that
reads smoke as a sign. Japan: *kōdō*, "listening to incense", heats aromatic wood on a mica plate precisely
to avoid smoke (S32); it is a reading of scent, not of smoke.

## 11. How science learned to read a plume

Leonardo's late *Deluge* drawings (c. 1517–18) show storm, water and debris in coiling motion; the Royal
Collection notes his interest in the optics of clouds, rain, water, dust and smoke (S33). Helmholtz (1858)
proved that vortex lines move with an ideal fluid, so a vortex ring persists (S34). In January 1867 Tait
built a crate with a round hole and a cloth back, filled with ammonia-and-acid smoke; striking the cloth
fired smoke rings across the room, and a month later Thomson (Kelvin) proposed in "On Vortex Atoms" that
atoms are such rings in the ether (S35). Tyndall lectured on sensitive flames in 1867 and later showed smoke
jets that respond to sound (S36). Reynolds (1883) watched a streak of dye in a pipe and found the speed at
which flow turns from direct to sinuous (S37).

The plume itself came later. Zel'dovich (1937) and W. Schmidt (1941) derived similarity laws for the
turbulent plume above a point source of heat (S38, S39; citations from secondary references, not read).
Batchelor (1954) reviewed buoyant convection (S40). Morton, Taylor and Turner (1956) closed the problem by
assuming that ambient air is drawn in at a rate proportional to the plume's own speed, and used it to
estimate how high smoke rises in stratified air (S41). Turner's *Buoyancy Effects in Fluids* (1973) is the
standard text (S42). My gloss, not a source claim: the page's "still" column is laminar, its "tower" that
breaks is the laminar-to-turbulent transition Reynolds described, its "ring" is a Helmholtz vortex, and the
widening of a turbulent plume is the entrainment of the 1956 model.
