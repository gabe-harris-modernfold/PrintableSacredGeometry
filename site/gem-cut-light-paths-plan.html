The Cut and the Critical Angle light paths through every standard gem cut, from a burning glass

A single self-contained page. The reader picks a cut and a material, aims a magnifying glass at it, and watches the ray tree bounce through the interior — where each ray totally internally reflects, where it leaks, and which facet it finally leaves by. Every angle on screen is computed from the declared geometry; the talismanic register is labelled and never feeds a number.

§0 Invariants

These are the constraints that make the page auditable. If a later decision breaks one of them, the decision is wrong, not the invariant.

Must hold on every frame

  1. Energy closes. Σ exited + Σ absorbed + Σ truncated = 1.000 ± 1e-9 for every launched ray. Displayed live, not just asserted.
  2. No acceleration structure. Geometry stays convex (or a small union of convex parts), so a ray inside hits exactly one facet and the tracer stays readable by eye. If a cut needs a BVH, the cut is out of scope.
  3. Angles are computed, never authored. Every θᵢ, θ_c and Fresnel coefficient on screen comes from the facet table and Sellmeier data at render time. No hardcoded "about 41 degrees" anywhere in the drawing code.
  4. The talismanic layer is inert. Vogel, Merkaba, Platonic and pyramid geometries are traced by exactly the same tracer as a princess cut. Attributed properties — prana, amplification, structuring — are text and cosmetic canvas only, and cannot feed a value back into the physics. Byte-identical isolation check, as in photon-quartz-simulation.html.
  5. Zero dependencies. Hand-written WebGL for the solid, 2D canvas overlay for annotation — the pattern already established by embryonic-torus-heart.html. No external <script src> anywhere in the repo, and that stays true.
  6. Two implementations, one truth. The 2D section tracer and the 3D kernel are separate code, and for any ray in a symmetry plane — where facet normals have no out-of-plane component and a 2D trace is exact, not approximate — they must agree to 1e-9 in positions, AOI sequence and intensities. The section view is citable because it is continuously proven against the solid, not because it is a tidy drawing.

§1 The result the page exists to show

This figure is the whole thesis, and it is already computed. That razor edge is the page's best teaching moment, and it generalises: the brilliant cut is not a universally good cut, it is a cut tuned to a high index. §2b The corridor — the brilliant path in closed form

For a vertical ray and an opposed pair of pavilion mains at angle p, the whole canonical path is three lines of arithmetic, and the tracer must reproduce them exactly — they are the cheapest strong asserts in the build:

  • first pavilion hit: AOI₁ = p
  • second pavilion hit: AOI₂ = 180° − 3p — two reflections off planes meeting at the culet compose to a rotation by 360° − 4p, which is where the next line comes from
  • back at the table: AOI₃ = |4p − 180°| — and at p = 45° exactly, the pavilion pair is a retroreflector and the ray leaves straight back up

Demanding TIR at both pavilion hits and an exit at the table pins p into a corridor, and the corridor is a property of the material alone:

Two things fall out of the algebra. First, the two-TIR window [θ_c, (180−θ_c)/3] is non-empty only while θ_c ≤ 45°, i.e. n ≥ √2 ≈ 1.4142 — below that index no pavilion angle can hold a vertical ray for two bounces, so “brilliant-cuttable” is a sharp material property, not a matter of degree. Fluorite (n 1.4338) squeaks in with a corridor barely a degree wide, which is why faceted fluorite is a collector's curiosity rather than a jewel. Second, Tolkowsky's 40.75° is an interior point of diamond's corridor with almost two degrees of slack, and a boundary point of quartz's — the §1 knife edge said in one sentence. The P8 crown-return scan must find its ridge inside these corridors — a computed check that the tracer and the algebra agree about where the good cuts live.

§3 Geometry engine — three primitives

Every entry in the roster reduces to one of three forms, which is what keeps the tracer small enough to audit.

1 · Half-space set → convex polyhedron

A cut is a table of facets, each {tilt from girdle plane, azimuth, offset}, and the solid is the intersection of those half-spaces. This covers every faceted cut in the roster. Because it is convex, a ray inside hits exactly one facet — the nearest positive-t plane — so there is no self-intersection class of bug and no traversal structure. Facets carry names (“pavilion main”, “upper girdle”, “star”) so the hit log reads like a gemologist's notes.

2 · Quadric → sphere, ellipsoid

Closed-form intersection. Covers cabochon, bead, egg and lens. The surface normal is analytic, so curved-surface hits carry the same Fresnel treatment as flat ones with no tessellation error.

3 · Small union of 1 and 2

Intersect the ray with each part, keep the nearest hit, and reject hits that fall inside another part. Only two roster entries need this — heart (the cleft) and merkaba (two interpenetrating tetrahedra) — so the part count stays at two or three and the fast path survives.

Consequence worth stating: because cuts are parameter sets rather than meshes, crown and pavilion angle become live sliders. The reader can walk a round brilliant off Tolkowsky's proportions and watch light return collapse, which no static diagram can teach.

From facet table to solid — the pipeline is part of the spec

A cut is declared as half-spaces {n̂ᵢ, dᵢ} generated from one symmetry wedge plus its point group. The pipeline: enumerate vertices as triple-plane intersections that satisfy every other half-space within ε; give each plane the vertices that lie on it, sorted angularly about the face centroid, as its polygon; then verify before anything is traced — V − E + F = 2, every edge shared by exactly two faces, every normal outward (n̂·(v − solid centroid) > 0), signed volume positive by the divergence theorem. Face areas and volume come free from the same pass, and volume × the §2 density is the carat readout. Symmetry is itself an assert twice over: the generated plane set must be invariant under its declared group to 1e-12 (catches azimuth typos at load), and the traced metrics at identity must inherit it — a round brilliant's crown return may not change under a 45° turn.

The girdle is an n-gon prism band (16 sides default) with a real thickness parameter; the scalloped girdle of a bruted stone is out of scope, and the notes say so. Every cut also declares its c-axis in gem frame — ∥ shaft for the wands and natural forms, table-normal by convention for faceted gems — because §4b's angle-to-c readout and the P6b quartz split are meaningless without it.

Fancy outlines — chord planes, not tilt tables

Ovals, pears, marquises and hearts have designed girdle outlines, so their facets cannot come from a tilt/azimuth table over a circle. The recipe that stays inside the half-space primitive: build the outline from circular arcs (an oval is the classic four-arc drafting construction; a marquise is two arcs meeting at points; a pear is three), sample it at the cut's azimuth boundaries, then define each pavilion main as the plane through its two girdle samples and the culet point — or the keel segment for elongated stones, where the culet stretches into a line and the two end planes close over it. Crown facets run the same chord construction to the table rim, and the table polygon is then derived by intersecting the crown planes with the table plane: if it fails to close, the proportions are wrong and the P1 gate catches it, not the reader. Step cuts are the identical recipe on concentric rectangles. This turns §10's “fancy girdles” risk from a modelling unknown into a data problem — which arcs, which radii — that P1 can source per cut.

Union semantics — one rule, stated once

For heart and merkaba the solid is a union of convex parts and the boundary test changes: take the nearest hit among parts, discard it if the point lies strictly inside any other part — an internal seam, same medium, no optical event — and re-march from t + ε. Membership in the union, not the part, decides whether an interface is real; a seam that refracts is the bug class this paragraph exists to kill. A bounce cap plus a strict progress check (every step advances t by more than ε) rules out the infinite-loop class. Edge and vertex grazes — two candidate planes within ε of the same t — resolve deterministically to the lower facet index and increment a degenerate-hit counter that must read zero across every §8 vector, because the pinned rays are chosen to be generic.

Depth budget — do not skip the girdle

Table 56% / crown 34.5° / pavilion 40.75° gives a total depth of 58.20% with a zero-thickness girdle, which sits below the GIA Excellent band of 59.0–62.3%. Real stones carry a girdle of roughly 2.5–4.5%, which lands them inside it. The girdle band must therefore be a modelled facet ring with its own thickness parameter, not an infinitely thin edge — otherwise every depth percentage the page reports will be wrong by about two points.

§3b The optics, written down — five equations and a ledger

Everything the tracer does at an interface is five lines. Writing them into the plan is what makes “angles are computed, never authored” a checkable claim instead of a slogan.

  1. Snell, vector form. With μ = n₁/n₂ and cos θₜ = √(1 − μ²(1 − cos²θᵢ)): t̂ = μ d̂ + (μ cos θᵢ − cos θₜ) n̂. Dots and square roots only — no trigonometry in the hot path, and every θ on screen is recovered from the same dot products the tracer used.
  2. TIR test. μ²(1 − cos²θᵢ) > 1. The reflection is d̂ − 2(d̂·n̂)n̂ either way.
  3. Fresnel amplitudes. r_s = (n₁cosθᵢ − n₂cosθₜ)/(n₁cosθᵢ + n₂cosθₜ), r_p = (n₂cosθᵢ − n₁cosθₜ)/(n₂cosθᵢ + n₁cosθₜ), and their t partners.
  4. Power, with the factor everyone forgets. R = |r|² but T = |t|² · (n₂cosθₜ)/(n₁cosθᵢ). Omit the projected-flux factor and energy quietly fails to close by exactly the amount that looks like a rendering bug. R + T = 1 is asserted per event, per polarization — this is the single most common Fresnel implementation error and the reason invariant №1 exists.
  5. TIR phase. tan(δ_s/2) = √(sin²θᵢ − n²ᵣₑₗ)/cosθᵢ and tan(δ_p/2) = the same root ÷ (n²ᵣₑₗ cosθᵢ). Lossless in power, but it converts linear polarization to elliptical — the §1 path's own second bounce in quartz (57.75°) applies Δ = 44.2°, within a degree of a Fresnel rhomb's 45°. The reference script pins the rhomb itself (n 1.51 at 54.62° → Δ = 45.00°) as the acceptance test for the formula.

The polarization ledger — Stokes vectors, Mueller events

Sunlight arrives unpolarized and incoherent, and after one oblique event it is neither fully polarized nor unpolarized. The only honest bookkeeping is a Stokes vector per ray segment with a Mueller matrix per event: a rotation into the event's s/p basis (the basis is event-local, and skipping the rotation between events is the classic wrong-numbers-that-look-right bug), a diattenuator built from R_s, R_p or T_s, T_p, and the TIR retarder from equation 5. Intensity is the ledger's first component, so invariant №1 is untouched; what the ledger buys is honesty — R_s ≠ R_p at every oblique bounce, and the mix entering bounce k depends on history — plus one genuinely new readout: the degree of polarization of the exit light. A stone under a burning glass is a weak polarizer, and this page can be the first place a reader sees that stated with numbers. Free asserts: an interior ray reflected at the internal Brewster angle (22.47° diamond, 32.92° quartz — both safely below θ_c, so the geometry actually occurs) comes off fully s-polarized, DOP = 1; every Mueller event conserves energy; and switching the ledger off must reproduce the unpolarized average (R_s + R_p)/2 exactly.

Beyond the interface

Absorption is Beer–Lambert per segment, I·e^(−α(λ)·ℓ) — effectively zero for the colourless defaults, and the hook by which ruby vs sapphire becomes a teaching pair: same index, same geometry, different α(λ), so colour changes not one angle on screen, only the spectrum. Dispersion is n(λ) per material: published Sellmeier coefficients where they exist (diamond, quartz o/e, sapphire o/e, fluorite), and for the rest a two-term Cauchy fit constructed to reproduce the pinned n_d and B–G dispersion exactly — stated per material in the data block, because presenting a fit as a measurement would be the one dishonest number on the page. Group vs phase: the §7b marker runs at c/n by design and says so; a physical pulse rides c/n_g with n_g = n − λ·dn/dλ ≈ 2.494 for diamond — 3.2% slower, one labelled line under the readout, and an assert that n_g > n across the visible for every material (normal dispersion throughout).

Deliberately out, and said once in the notes rather than hidden: diffraction (facets are 10³–10⁴ wavelengths across — geometric optics is the correct theory, not a shortcut), interference between branches (millimetre path differences against micron coherence; photon-quartz-simulation.html already computes that washout and is linked, not re-derived), and frustrated TIR (nothing sits within a wavelength of a facet).

§4 The source — a real burning glass

The instrument is pinned: f = 100 mm, Ø 75 mm — f/1.33, a real hand burning glass. The sun subtends 9.30 mrad, so the smallest spot this lens can make is f·θ☉ = 0.930 mm — a floor set by the angular size of the sun, not a number the reader can dial past. (A 1.000 mm solar image would need f = 107.5 mm; that is trivia, the floor is physics.) §4b Frames — the stone turns, the light does not

The burning glass is fixed in world space. The reader rotates the gem, and the interior paths and exit rays move as a consequence. This is how a stone is actually examined — tilted under a fixed light — and it makes orientation the primary independent variable of the whole page, not a view setting.

Trace in gem space, pull the source through M⁻¹

Two frames, never conflated: the gem frame, where the facet table lives, and the world frame, where the lens, spot and viewer live. The rotation M maps one to the other. The tracer runs entirely in gem space, and the only per-frame transform is pulling the fixed source rays through M⁻¹. Three reasons this direction and not the other:

  • The facet table is never mutated, so repeated rotation accumulates no floating-point drift into the geometry.
  • The §8 acceptance vectors are defined in gem space, so they stay directly comparable at any orientation instead of only at identity.
  • Interior paths come out in gem space, which is exactly the frame the WebGL model matrix already draws in — so the 3D view needs no extra transform at all.

Accumulate orientation as a quaternion, renormalised each frame — Euler angles will gimbal-lock on a stone the reader is free to tumble. Provide an exact reset to identity, because the load-time asserts run at identity and must be reachable without drift.

Camera and gem are different controls

Rotating the gem changes the physics. Orbiting the camera changes nothing but the view. Conflating them would make the page unreadable, so: drag rotates the stone, modifier-drag orbits the camera, and the two readouts are visually distinct. This gives a clean invariant to assert — orbiting the camera must leave every computed readout byte-identical, the same isolation discipline as the talismanic layer.

Why a high index is forgiving — the interior cone is capped at θ_c

Refraction compresses tilt. Entering the table of a diamond, a 40° external swing becomes only 15.42° of interior swing. Push the external angle to its limit and the identity is exact:

sin ψ_max = sin 90° / n = 1/n, so ψ_max = θ_c. The interior ray can never sit further than the critical angle from the entry facet's normal, however the stone is turned — 24.43° in diamond, 40.36° in quartz.

That is the real reason a brilliant-cut diamond returns light across a wide range of orientations: the whole hemisphere of possible entry directions is folded into a 24.43° interior cone, and the 40.75° pavilion is chosen to sit safely outside it. The arithmetic also states the failure mode honestly — worst case, a grazing entry ray meets the pavilion at 40.75° − 24.43° = 16.32°, which is 8.11° below diamond's critical angle and therefore leaks. Even diamond windows at extreme tilt. Note that 16.32° is the same number as diamond's pavilion margin in §2; it is the same subtraction seen from the other end.

Collimated leak-onset, by contrast, is section arithmetic, closed-form and pinned now: leak begins when the interior tilt ψ = asin(sin τ/n) eats the pavilion margin. For quartz that is an external tilt of just 0.61° — the 0.39° margin translated into hand terms: under collimated light, a quartz brilliant starts windowing within the wobble of tweezers. Diamond holds to 42.78° of tilt, and its second bounce can never fail from table entry, because 57.75° − θ_c = 33.3° exceeds the 24.43° interior cone cap — the §4b identity closing its own loop. Quartz's second bounce fails beyond 27.49°. The tilt at which the full f/1.33 cone begins to leak remains a genuine Phase 5 deliverable — these collimated anchors are its acceptance brackets, not its answer.

Orientation readouts

  • Currently illuminated facet, named, with its live AOI
  • Tilt and azimuth, plus angle to the c-axis — required for quartz birefringence and for the Vogel cut, both of which are defined relative to it
  • Light-return signature — crown return plotted against orientation, traced out as the stone turns. This is what sparkle actually is, quantified: a stone's signature over orientation rather than a single number at rest. Probably the most original readout on the page.

§4c Convergence, in and out

Everything so far stays inside the girdle. Two things worth showing sit outside it: where the burning glass itself converges the light before the table, and where the exit fan's rays converge — or fail to — after it. Both are free reads of numbers already on the page, pulled forward as a static preview rather than held back for Phase 5's full interactive treatment.

The lens, at focus — a static preview of §4

§4 already pins the instrument: f = 100 mm, Ø 75 mm, focus at standoff s = f, giving the marginal-ray half-angle ψ = atan((D/2)/f) = 20.56° and the 0.930 mm sun-disk spot — the same ±20.56° that reappears in §4's own cone table. Drawing the lens and its converging marginal rays at this one fixed standoff costs nothing new: no aperture sampling, no standoff slider, no rotation — those stay Phase 5. What it buys now is the picture the reader is missing without it: the stone sits at the tip of a cone closing from a 75 mm circle down to under a millimetre, and that cone, not the stone, is what decides how much light arrives and at what angular spread.

Scale is the honest complication. A girdle normalised to 100 units reads as 6.5 mm (§7b's "about one carat"), so 1 unit = 0.065 mm and the lens sits f / 0.065 ≈ 1539 girdle-units above the table — thirty-odd stone diameters away. Drawn at the same scale as the section figure the lens would be off the page; drawn at the lens's own scale the stone is a speck. Both facts are real, so the view is a zoom preset, not a slider: gem (§1's tight crop, unchanged), exit zone (below), and full bench, which shows the true 1539-unit separation and lets the stone read as small as it actually is next to a 75 mm lens. Nothing here is schematised to make the picture prettier.

The exit fan — mostly parallel, not a focus

The tempting picture is a lens-like focal point on the far side of the stone. The corridor algebra of §2b says otherwise, and it is worth stating plainly because it is surprising: AOI₃ = |4p − 180°| depends only on the pavilion angle p, not on where the ray entered, and the terminal facet's normal is fixed — so every ray in the fan that exits through the same facet leaves exactly parallel to every other, merely translated sideways by its own entry offset. Parallel rays never converge, at any distance. A round brilliant, illuminated by a collimated beam, is not a lens.

The central pair is a false lead worth naming, because it is the intuitive guess and it is wrong: a ray entering just left of the axis and its mirror just right of it have already swapped sides inside the stone — pav-main → pav-main → table sends the left entrant out the right-hand side of the table, and vice versa — so by the time both are back in air they are opening away from each other, not closing. Their forward paths never meet; the computation must say so plainly rather than assume a crossing that isn't there. The real crossings sit where the terminal hit's facet changes — table to crown main, per the §4b handoff table — because that is where the exit direction actually jumps, and only there. A second, purely geometric fact falls out for free: the two pavilion-main bounces are plain reflection, angle-in equals angle-out, with no index in the formula — so where (which entry offset) that facet handoff happens is identical for every material. Only the crossing distance, set by the final Snell exit at that hit, depends on n. The right computation is therefore not "find the focus" but find where adjacent rays in the fan actually intersect: extend each exit ray as a line, solve each adjacent pair for its crossing parameter, and keep only the crossings that fall in front of both rays (t > 0 for each). What survives is a short, mirrored list of real crossing points — a caustic in the literal sense, not a focus — reported as a distance from the girdle in millimetres (same 0.065 mm/unit convention as above), with every other adjacent pair stated as "parallel, no finite crossing" rather than silently omitted. gem_trace_reference.py carries the exact figures once computed, per the usual convention of this plan: numbers appear here only after the audit script has produced them.

Constraints this addition must hold: the lens preview draws from §4's already-pinned f, D and spot with no new inputs invented for the picture; the caustic search is exhaustive over adjacent fan pairs, not sampled, so it cannot miss a crossing between two rays that are actually in the fan; a parallel run (no finite crossing) and a real crossing must both be represented on screen, never merged into one silent "no data" state; and zoom is a small enum — gem / exit zone / full bench — with each preset's framing computed from the same geometry rather than three hand-tuned viewports.

§6 The Vogel result

The Vogel cut earns its place because it makes a geometric claim a ray tracer can test — but the spec itself is ambiguous, and the honest move is to model both readings rather than choose one silently. The tradition ties the receptive tip to the Great Pyramid's 51°51′51″. Read as the included apex angle (a slender needle), each facet lies 25.93° off the c-axis and its normal 64.07° off — which is the AOI for a ray travelling straight down the axis. Read with the pyramid's own semantics — the facet rising 51°51′51″ from the base plane, exactly as the pyramid face rises from the ground — the tip is blunter and the axial AOI is 51.86°. Reading B has three things going for it: it is what the pyramid comparison literally says; it matches the convention that the receptive end is blunter than the transmitting end; and it lands 4.8 arcminutes from the r-face of a natural quartz termination — 51.78° from the basal plane, computed from the cell parameters a 4.9137 Å, c 5.4047 Å — so a reading-B Vogel is a regularised natural point, which is very likely what Vogel was looking at. Default to B, offer A as a toggle, print which is active on every readout.

Under both readings the headline stands, and it is not the one the tradition states: a sharp quartz termination traps axial light rather than projecting it. The natural-termination row upgrades the claim from a fact about Vogel wands to a fact about every quartz point ever grown: the crystal's own r-face angle of 51.78° already exceeds θ_c by 11.4°, which is why a quartz point glows along its length instead of beaming from the tip. The one honest leak in the story is the focused cone against a reading-B tip — 6.8% of the fan arrives under critical and transmits — and the page should show that too, because it is the difference between a trapped ray and a trapped beam. The tradition's “amplified every time it hits a facet” maps onto “totally internally reflected at every facet, losslessly” — the redirection is real and the page should show it beautifully; the amplification is not, and the Σ = 1.000 readout will say so on every frame. That is the house rule working exactly as intended: the physics computed live, the interpretation labelled, neither pretending to be the other.

The Star of David pendant — the same test, flat

The Vogel Star of David is the wand's flat sibling, and the sources describe something more specific than a hexagram-shaped stone: a compact, generally hexagonal quartz pendant whose two broad faces carry opposed triangular reliefs — an upright triangle cut comparatively flat on the front (“male/transmitting”, worn outward) and an inverted triangle cut deeper, “fatter”, into the back (“female/receptive”, worn toward the body), rotated 60° (Satya Center's product description; Luminary Studios — a cutter who worked with Vogel — cautions that much of what is marketed as “Vogel” does not follow the original standards, so bevel angles ship approximate per §10, with the retailer-measured thickness ≈ 0.375× triangle edge kept). The stone is not a six-pointed silhouette: the hexagram a wearer sees is the through-thickness overlap of the two triangle boundaries in projection through the transparent quartz — an optical composite, which is also why refraction makes the rear triangle appear to shift slightly as the viewing angle changes. That is still an exact fact the tracer draws from the facet table rather than asserts: two equilateral triangles about one axis, 60° apart, project six vertices onto one circle at 60° spacing, alternating front/back. (An earlier draft of this section modelled the pendant as the two triangles' convex hull — a triangular antiprism, the regular octahedron at equilateral proportion. That solid gets the projection theorem right and the object wrong: it is front-back symmetric, erasing exactly the male/female asymmetry the sources treat as essential, and it has no pendant body at all.)

The pendant also sharpens the register boundary, in both directions. The tradition's own wording for the receptive angle — an “internal angle of 51°51′51″” that “mirrors the angle of the sides of the Great Pyramid” — uses reading A's noun and reading B's comparison in a single sentence, which is exactly the ambiguity the toggle above models; the page can quote the sentence and show both. C-axis alignment is declared per cut and feeds the §4b angle-to-c readout and the P6b split; piezoelectricity is a true property of quartz — but it couples charge to strain, not to the light paths traced here, so it lives in the labelled register as a true-and-orthogonal fact. “Energy spirals against the internal facets” maps onto something the tracer genuinely shows: skew rays in a hexagonal cross-section TIR around the axis in helical paths, and the 3D view will draw those spirals live. The spiral is real; the “amplifying with each bounce” is not, and Σ = 1.000 says so on every frame. As for the “concentrated, laser-like beam” from the male tip — the tables above are the tracer's answer: a tip sharper than 99.29° included cannot transmit axial light at all, and the honest observable is the 6.8% cone leak under reading B. Geometric optics also has no mechanism for coherence; “beam coherence” across facet counts is not a quantity this page can measure, and the notes say so rather than gesture at it.

What survives a change — the rule that makes comparison work

Changing the cut keeps material, orientation, spot, view and comparator. Changing the material keeps all of that plus the cut. Nothing resets anything else, ever. Every comparison on the page depends on holding all other variables fixed, so a control that quietly restores a default destroys the page's core workflow. This deserves an assert, not just care.

Two consequences that are easy to miss:

  • Snap orientations must be generated per cut, not hardcoded. “crown-normal 34.50°” is a fact about a round brilliant; a step cut's crown angle differs, and a briolette, rose cut or sphere has no table to be “table-up” about. The snap list is derived from the current facet table and renamed on every cut change.
  • The rotation pivot is a stated parameter. Where the stone turns about decides where the fixed spot lands as it rotates. The §4b handoff figures assume the girdle centre; the centroid is arguably more natural for a stone held in tweezers. Pick one, write it down, and keep the handoff table consistent with it.

Size: the one place scale actually matters

Cuts differ in proportion, so swapping one raises a question the plan had not asked — does the stone keep the same girdle diameter, or the same weight? Snell, Fresnel and TIR depend only on directions and indices, so crown return is scale-invariant for a transparent stone. Two things are not:

  • Absorption is exponential in path length, so a coloured stone's numbers move with size.
  • Spot-to-stone ratio. A 0.93 mm spot is a large fraction of a 3 mm stone and a small one on a 10 mm stone, which changes how many facets are lit and how much of the f/1.33 cone enters at all.

So offer both normalisations and label which is active: equal carat weight — equal mass, not equal volume; volume follows from the §2 density column, which is why a one-carat CZ at ρ 5.68 is a visibly smaller stone than a one-carat diamond at 3.52, and why the trade sells CZ by size instead — or equal girdle diameter, which keeps the section figures visually comparable. Default to equal weight, because that is the comparison a reader means when they ask which cut performs better; the earlier draft glossed carat as “equal volume”, which is only true within a single material.

Section is the primary view, not 3D

A deliberate reversal of what the phase order implied. The section is the figure gemological literature actually uses: unambiguous, citable, and the only view where θᵢ against θ_c is legible per bounce. 3D is more impressive and harder to read precisely, so it is a peer view the reader switches to — never the thing they must fight through first.

Five views, one tracer

The tracer runs once per frame; every view is a projection of the same ray tree, so they cannot disagree.

  • Section — the plane of §1, live and labelled. Default.
  • 3D orbit — translucent solid in hand-written WebGL, ray tree as line segments with opacity tracking intensity, facet under the cursor named.
  • Facet heat map — energy incident on and leaked through each named facet, unrolled flat. Where a marquise bow-tie becomes a visible pattern rather than an anecdote.
  • Exit map — hemispherical plot of where light leaves, binned to table, crown, girdle, pavilion, with an optional ASET-style colouring by exit elevation for readers arriving from gemology (labelled as an homage, not a calibrated ASET).
  • Signature — crown return against orientation, per §4b. The only view that needs the stone in motion to mean anything.

Every number carries a comparator

“61.4% crown return” alone is unreadable — the reader has no scale. Each metric therefore ships with a reference value: the same cut in the best-performing material, or the best-in-class cut in the current material, selectable. This single decision is most of what separates a reference from a demo.

  • % returned through the crown — brilliance
  • % leaked through the pavilion — windowing / fish-eye
  • mean TIR count before exit, plus the distribution
  • angular spread of exit rays, and dispersion spread across the 7 wavelengths — fire
  • degree of polarization of the exit light — the §3b Stokes ledger's readout, off by default, and the page's one measurement a reader has likely never seen quoted for a gem
  • Σ exited + Σ absorbed + Σ truncated, displayed to prove closure

Reference surfaces

  • Compare (A/B). Two cuts side by side, or one cut in two materials, at identical orientation and spot. The shared state is the point — a comparison where the two panes drift apart is worthless.
  • All 37. Sortable table of the whole roster with computed return, leak and mean TIR for the current material, filterable by family. Click a row to open that stone live. This is the surface that answers “which cut does what” without 36 visits.
  • Silhouette picker — specified above. It shares the all-37 cache, so build the cache first and both surfaces fall out of it.
  • Hit log. One row per bounce of the selected ray: facet name, θᵢ, θ_c, R_s/R_p, T, TIR phase where it applies, cumulative intensity, verdict. Copyable as text. This is the readout that answers the original question directly — where the light is quartered, halved, dimmed, and where it finally leaves. It has real stories to tell even for the ideal stone: the §8 diamond ledger's fourth bounce meets the pavilion at 23.75°, a mere 0.68° under critical, and sheds 64% of the remainder through a 76.8° grazing window — perfection lasting exactly three bounces.

Entry points

37 cuts, 9 materials and five sliders with nothing to do first is paralysis. Presets that pose a question and land on the state that answers it — “why does glass leak where quartz holds?”, “the quartz knife edge”, “what a step cut does instead”, “does a Vogel point transmit?”. This follows the house pattern rather than inventing one: frequency-ladder-teaching-tool.html pairs a guided ladder with Free Play, and mineral-lattices-platonic-solids.html runs nine plates. Presets are shortcuts into the live tool, not a separate guided mode — the reader keeps every control the moment they land.

Citability is a feature, not a shipping chore

  • Deep links. Full state in the URL hash — cut, material, orientation, spot, view, comparator. The quartz knife edge should be a URL. Built early, in P4, because the presets are just deep links and testing needs them.
  • Named snap orientations. Free tumble is right for exploring and wrong for citing, because an arbitrary quaternion is not reproducible by hand. Snaps — table-up, crown-normal 34.50°, girdle-on, c-axis aligned — plus numeric tilt/azimuth entry.
  • Export. Hit log and metrics as copyable text, section figure as SVG. Without this the page is a dead end for anyone doing real work with it.

Animate pavilion angle across its band and watch crown return collapse continuously. For quartz, with only 0.39° of margin, the return should visibly die inside a fraction of a degree — the knife edge of §1 turned into a motion the reader feels rather than reads.

Rules

  • Determinism. Tracer output must not depend on animation time. The ray tree is computed from geometry and source state alone; animation only controls what is drawn. Otherwise the load-time asserts become frame-dependent and worthless.
  • The marker is not a photon. It is a phase marker on a ray path, and the label must say so — carrying forward the markers-are-not-photons invariant from photon-quartz-simulation.html.
  • Never the only channel. Every value an animation conveys is also available as a static number. Motion is emphasis, never the sole carrier.
  • Honour prefers-reduced-motion. All four stop; the page falls back to the fully drawn ray tree with the hit log intact, losing nothing but the pacing.
  • Pause and scrub. The wavefront marker needs a scrub control, because the interesting frames are the bounces and they pass quickly at any watchable speed.

§8 Validation

Python is the auditor, not the runtime — the same division of labour as dodeca_frame.py and the photon sim's 66 load-time asserts. A companion gem_trace_reference.py — pure numpy, no trimesh, because the auditor must be an independent implementation of the same three primitives rather than a different library's opinion — traces a fixed set of rays, dumps expected hit points, intensities and Stokes states to JSON, and the page asserts against them on load. Invariants to assert on load

  • Energy closure per ray, to 1e-9 — with the §3b projected-flux factor, per polarization
  • Reversibility — reversing an exit ray retraces the entry path
  • Rotation invariance. For a random orientation M, tracing with the source pulled through M⁻¹ must produce the same interior AOI sequence as tracing at identity with the source rotated by M⁻¹ directly. This is the test that catches transform-plumbing errors, which are otherwise invisible — the picture looks plausible at every orientation while the numbers are quietly wrong.
  • 2D/3D agreement. Symmetry-plane rays traced by the 3D kernel match the section tracer to 1e-9 — invariant №6 of §0, exercised on every load
  • Interior cone cap — for entry at grazing incidence, the refracted angle equals θ_c, for every material. Cheap, and it pins the §4b identity.
  • Corridor identities — at three pavilion angles the traced AOI sequence reproduces AOI₂ = 180° − 3p and AOI₃ = |4p − 180°| exactly, and the two-TIR window empties at n = √2 (§2b)
  • Camera isolation — orbiting the camera changes no computed readout
  • Every roster geometry is closed — V − E + F = 2, two faces per edge, outward normals, positive volume — and its declared symmetry group maps its plane set to itself (1e-12)
  • Platonic dihedrals from the generated solids match closed form (70.5288°, 90°, 109.4712°, 116.5651°, 138.1897°) to 1e-9 — the geometry kernel audited against textbook values it never contains
  • θ_c recomputed from n matches the material table for all 9 materials, and each material's n(λ) reproduces its pinned n_d to 1e-4 and B–G dispersion to 5e-4
  • Fresnel R → 1 continuously as θᵢ → θ_c from below (no discontinuity at the boundary), and R_p vanishes at Brewster — a reflected interior ray at 22.47° (diamond) reads DOP = 1 off the Stokes ledger
  • Mueller events conserve energy; ledger off reproduces (R_s + R_p)/2 exactly; n_g > n across the visible for every material
  • Degenerate-hit counter reads zero across all pinned vectors
  • Carat readout for the round brilliant agrees with the trade estimate d² · depth · 0.0061 within 5% — a sanity bracket, not a law
  • Talismanic layer isolation: toggling it leaves every computed readout byte-identical

§10 Known risks

Facet data is the schedule

Nine of the ten phases are tractable engineering. Phase 1 is not — published proportions for oval, pear, marquise, princess, radiant, cushion, trilliant and the antique cuts are scattered across lapidary references of uneven reliability, and several are trade conventions rather than standards. Expect to cite ranges instead of single values, and expect the fancy shapes to need a hand-built girdle outline that a single tilt/azimuth table cannot express. If a cut cannot be sourced credibly it should ship marked as approximate rather than quietly guessed.

  • Ray count against frame rate. A branching tree with spectral sampling multiplies fast: 7 wavelengths × aperture samples × 2 branches per hit. Needs an intensity floor and a bounce cap chosen by measurement, and a “render once at high quality” path for stills.
  • Birefringence is now a phase, not a hope. Quartz's o/e split is exactly what puts it on the 0.39° knife edge, so it is promoted to P6b with a stated model and gates. The photon sim models the split for a flat block and is the reference; the faceted extension is real work, which is why P6b carries a kill-switch — ship the o-ray trace with the e-extreme quoted as a bracket — rather than an assumption that it lands.
  • The critical angle is a square-root edge. T ∝ √(θ_c − θ) near the boundary — T climbs from 11% to 50% across the last 0.4° in glass — so uniform angular sampling puts its largest error exactly where the page's story lives. The cone integrator needs adaptive refinement (or an analytic sub-integral) in a ~1° band around θ_c per material, and the §4 survival fractions are the regression test that says whether it worked.
  • Fancy-shape girdles. The chord-plane recipe of §3 settles the method; the remaining risk is data — which arcs, which radii, which azimuth boundaries per cut. Source the outline constructions in Phase 1 alongside the angles, and ship any cut whose outline cannot be sourced credibly as marked-approximate rather than quietly guessed.
  • The all-37 table costs 37 traces. Populating it means tracing every cut in the roster at the current material and orientation, which cannot run at interactive ray counts. It needs a reduced-quality pass with the reduction stated on the table, and a cache keyed on material and orientation. A silently down-sampled reference table that reads as authoritative is worse than no table.
  • Numbers in this plan are pre-tracer. Everything quoted here is closed-form 2D arithmetic, re-derived 2026-08-03 by the plan-audit script that succeeded anchor_check.py / section_trace.py (which lived in a session scratchpad and are gone — the P1 gem_trace_reference.py makes them permanent). These checks keep paying rent: the first round caught a direction-only derivation comparing a ray against the crown facet on the wrong side; this round caught a mislabelled dispersion convention with a flint value on a crown glass, a sapphire row mixing o- and e-indices, a Vogel cone span that skipped refraction compression at entry, an equal-carat note that conflated mass with volume, and a monotonicity gate that was wrong physics. They are still not a substitute for the 3D tracer agreeing with them.
The physics is computed live and the interpretation is labelled — the rule this page inherits, and the only reason the talismanic cuts can share a tracer with a princess cut without either register contaminating the other.
