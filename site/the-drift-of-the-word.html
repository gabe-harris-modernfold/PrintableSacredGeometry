The Drift of the Word

a measured family tree of Christianity, from the Nile to the newsfeed

Every idea that reaches you has been copied, translated, argued over and retold many times. Each retelling keeps most of what it received and changes a little. Cultural evolution, the science of how knowledge passes between people, has mathematics for this. It came from population genetics and information theory. This page applies that mathematics to one of the longest, best-documented chains of transmission there is: the ideas that became Christianity, from the religions of Egypt, Canaan, Persia and Greece, through Israel and the early church, to the denominations of the last five centuries and the search box.

What this page measures, and what it doesn’t

It measures transmission: how much of a text, an idea, an attribution or a practice survives a passage from one community to the next. It does not measure truth, and it does not treat change as corruption. Most traditions have their own account of legitimate development (see §VI). The drift numbers come from a stated model with stated parameters, and you can change every one of them. The evidence grades on each link come from the historical record and are the part to take most seriously.

I · The instrumentFour things can drift, and one thing stops it

An idea is not one thing. Its wording can survive while its meaning changes. Its meaning can survive while everyone forgets where it came from. A rite can be performed faithfully for centuries by people who no longer hold the belief it once expressed. So the model tracks four axes separately, because each kind of medium protects a different one.

TextThe wording itself: the sayings, verses and formulas, as units that can be copied right or wrong.
MeaningWhat the words are understood to say, compared with what the originator meant.
ProvenanceKnowing where it came from: who said it, when, and drawing on whom.
PracticeThe lived part: the rite, the custom, the way of doing it.

Along each link of the tree, a unit is copied once per generation. It goes wrong with probability μ. With probability c, the copier goes back to a fixed source, such as a written scripture, a reference manuscript or a liturgical book, instead of copying the previous copy. Some links also carry a one-time event such as a translation, a council or a borrowing between religions. That event multiplies in a retention factor e. On a borrowing link e is small by design. It is the share of the whole source tradition that crosses over: Proverbs took about thirty sayings from Egypt, not Egyptian religion.

fraction surviving one link, after g generations: f(g) = f* + (1 − f*) · a^g a = (1 − μ)(1 − c) f* = (1 − μ)·c / (1 − a) link retention r = f(g) · e drift to a node = 1 − ∏ r (product along the strongest path)

The behaviour that matters is in c. With no source to go back to (c = 0), the plateau f* is zero and the idea decays exponentially for ever. With any access at all, decay stops at a plateau. The error rate sets how fast an idea drifts. Whether the source can still be reached sets whether drift ever stops. Try it below.

II · The treeThirty-nine communities, seventy-one links, a number on each

This is not a straight line from Egypt to the Greeks to the Jews. The record shows several streams converging. Israel grew mostly out of Canaanite culture. Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia and Greece flow in as influences, at different times and with very different strengths of evidence. Solid lines are descent, where a community inherits from its parent. Dashed lines are borrowing between traditions. Dotted lines are anchors: manuscripts that lay buried and skipped centuries of copying. Each line’s colour gives its evidence grade.

III · The shared-error testTelling inheritance from coincidence

Two traditions can share a form for two reasons. One copied the other, or both arrived at the same form independently, because some forms are what human minds and hands keep producing. The two cases look the same from a distance. The test that separates them comes from textual criticism, from Karl Lachmann’s method of reconstructing lost manuscripts: shared errors prove shared descent. Anyone can independently arrive at a mother nursing a child, or at the idea that the dead will rise. Nobody independently arrives at the same odd number, the same misreading or the same misplaced verse.

Each claim below gets the same three questions. Is there a shared idiosyncrasy? Is there a documented route of contact? Does the source come first in time?

IV · What is actually measuredThe numbers that are not a model

The tree’s drift numbers are modelled. Some numbers in this history are measured, and they calibrate the model. The most striking is the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran: copied about 125 BCE, buried, and compared in 1947 with the medieval Hebrew text. More than a thousand years of copying sat between the two, and they turned out to be the same book, differing mostly in spelling and small points of wording. That is scribal copying with a strong anchor, the plateau regime of §I seen in real data.

V · The ruler, and how to bend itEvery parameter on the table

The tree’s numbers rest on five channel presets and one event factor per link. They are judgements, set to be roughly consistent with the measured figures above, and they are not sacred. Bend them and watch the tree. What changes is the size of the numbers. What holds is their order: translation and borrowing cost meaning, anchors save text, and the Egyptian contribution is thin on every setting.

VI · The tradition’s own drift theoryDevelopment or corruption?

Christianity has argued about drift from the start, and its own answers line up with the model’s terms surprisingly well. In 1845 John Henry Newman, an Anglican on his way to Rome, wrote An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine. He asked the question this page asks: how can you tell when a teaching has grown and when it has decayed? He proposed seven “notes” of genuine development. Read them against the model:

Newman’s note (1845)The model’s term
Preservation of typeAn attractor: the form keeps returning to the same shape even as details change
Continuity of principlesThe meaning axis: retention of the core sense across links
Power of assimilationBorrowing links: taking in Greek philosophy without being dissolved by it
Logical sequenceChange in a consistent direction, not random drift
Anticipation of its futureEarly traces of the later form, as evidence of descent
Conservative action upon its pastThe anchor term c: a development must not overturn its source
Chronic vigourSurvival: the branch keeps producing descendants and doesn’t end

The great divisions can be read as different bets on which axis to protect. The Reformers’ sola scriptura is a bet on the text axis: raise c by going back to the Greek and Hebrew, past a thousand years of commentary. Orthodoxy bets on practice. Its liturgy keeps a form whose core is traditionally dated to the late fourth century, and its theology lives inside that worship. Catholicism bets on an interpreting authority, a living c in the form of a teaching office rather than a fixed text. Restorationist movements bet on provenance, claiming direct return to the first church, and often move fastest on meaning. Rabbinic Judaism, the other surviving descendant of Second Temple Judaism, made the oral commentary itself part of the anchor.

None of these bets is a mistake. Each one protects one axis at the cost of another. That is the main finding of the model, here in the tradition’s own history: no channel prevents drift. A channel only chooses which axis absorbs it.

VII · LimitsWhere the instrument stops

The nodes are my selection. Thirty-nine communities stand for thousands. A different cut (Syriac Christianity, the Church of the East, African independent churches) would change some paths. The tree is a lens, not an atlas.

Meaning has no natural ruler. Text drift can be counted against manuscripts. Meaning drift here is a judgement put into numbers so it can be compared and argued with. It shouldn’t be quoted as a finding.

The strongest path is not the only path. An idea that reaches a node by three weak routes may be better preserved than one strong route suggests. The model reports the best single channel, a lower bound on what survives.

Survivorship. We measure the branches that left witnesses. The Jewish Christians and the Gnostics are on the tree only because a few texts survived. Countless local forms left none.

Drift is not decline. A low meaning-retention number says the idea changed, not that it got worse. Insiders may call the same change development, inspiration or the work of the Spirit. History cannot settle that question, and this page does not try.
